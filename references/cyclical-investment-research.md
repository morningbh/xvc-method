# Cyclical Investment Research

Use this reference when the research question involves cyclical industries, commodity-like pricing, supply-demand imbalance, capacity expansion, inventory cycles, pricing power, or market-implied earnings.

Examples:
- memory, semiconductors, panels, solar, batteries, lithium, copper, chemicals, shipping, airlines, energy, agriculture, real estate, cloud capacity, GPU supply chains

## Core keystone question

Do not start with "is the industry good?"

Start with:

What duration and magnitude of pricing, margin, or volume cycle is already implied by current market price, and can the real supply-demand mechanism sustain more than that?

## What-has-to-be-true chain for cyclical equities

`current stock price is attractive`
`-> market-implied cycle duration or peak earnings is too conservative`
`-> price / volume / margin / mix must stay stronger for longer than implied`
`-> supply response must be delayed or demand must remain durable`
`-> inventory and customer behavior must not reveal pull-forward demand`
`-> leading indicators should preserve the thesis`

## Required decomposition

### 1. Market-implied expectation
Research:
- current market cap and enterprise value
- consensus revenue, EPS, EBITDA, FCF
- historical peak, trough, and mid-cycle earnings
- normalized earnings versus peak earnings
- current multiple on peak, trough, and normalized earnings
- what duration of elevated pricing or margin is required for current valuation to work

Kill signals:
- current price already requires extreme peak earnings or unusually long cycle duration
- upside depends almost entirely on terminal multiple expansion
- normalized earnings valuation is not attractive even if peak earnings looks cheap

### 2. Historical cycle structure
Research:
- past price cycles: magnitude, duration, and sequence
- margin cycle: trough, recovery, peak, decline
- volume cycle and demand shock behavior
- inventory cycle and customer stockpiling behavior
- capex response and lag
- management behavior in prior upcycles
- how previous cycles ended

Output fields:
- cycle start
- cycle peak
- cycle end
- price change
- margin change
- capex response
- inventory response
- demand driver
- supply response lag
- trigger of downturn

### 3. Supply response mechanism
Research:
- expansion lead time
- technology migration lead time
- equipment bottlenecks
- yield learning curve
- capacity fungibility across products
- competitor discipline
- financing constraints
- regulatory or geopolitical constraints

Key question:
If demand stays strong, how long until supply can realistically respond?

Kill signals:
- capacity can respond quickly enough to cap pricing
- competitors are already expanding aggressively
- bottlenecks are not binding
- supply is more fungible than the bullish thesis assumes

### 4. Demand durability
Research:
- structural demand versus cyclical rebound
- true consumption versus inventory build
- customer capex and usage behavior
- cancellation risk
- contract quality and price mechanisms
- end-market elasticity
- replacement cycle
- concentration in a few customers or use cases

Key question:
How much demand is real consumption, and how much is pull-forward, panic buying, stockpiling, or financial commitment that could reverse?

Kill signals:
- demand is mostly inventory build
- customers have weak contractual commitment
- customer capex or usage data turns down
- downstream inventory rises while orders remain high

### 5. Cycle extension or break mechanism
Research both paths:

Extension path:
- demand lasts longer than historical cycles
- supply response is structurally slower
- product mix shifts toward constrained higher-margin products
- contracts or ecosystem constraints reduce cyclicality

Break path:
- customers over-order
- competitors expand
- prices fall before earnings peak
- marginal demand is elastic
- market has already capitalized the peak

### 6. Falsification indicators
Track:
- spot and contract prices
- lead times
- order cancellation or deferral
- customer inventory
- supplier inventory
- capex announcements
- utilization
- competitor guidance
- gross margin guidance
- consensus EPS revisions
- customer capex revisions
- downstream demand signals

## Minimal first-pass test

When time is limited, run this first:

1. Reverse-engineer what current price implies for peak earnings, normalized earnings, margin, and cycle duration.
2. Compare that implied duration with historical cycles and current expansion lead times.
3. Check whether current demand evidence indicates consumption or inventory build.
4. Define 5 to 8 leading indicators that would kill the cycle-extension thesis.

## Output template

| link | research question | fastest evidence | kill signal | preserve signal | escalate trigger |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| price | what cycle is priced in? | valuation reverse-engineering | extreme implied duration | modest implied duration | consensus uncertainty |
| history | what is the base rate? | historical price/margin/capex cycles | current cycle already exceeds base rate without new mechanism | cycle still within plausible range | history not comparable |
| supply | how fast can supply respond? | capex, lead time, equipment, yield | supply response already visible | bottlenecks remain binding | private supply data needed |
| demand | is demand real or pull-forward? | customer capex, inventory, contracts | inventory/pull-forward evidence | consumption evidence | customer checks needed |
| indicators | how will it fail? | price, lead time, inventory, guidance | early reversal signals | indicators remain supportive | mixed data |

## Common mistakes

Avoid:
- treating strong current pricing as enough evidence
- using peak-cycle earnings as normalized earnings
- relying on management commentary without checking incentives
- missing inventory build or pull-forward demand
- confusing structural demand with temporary bottleneck
- ignoring the market-implied duration of the cycle
- doing broad industry research before reverse-engineering price expectations
